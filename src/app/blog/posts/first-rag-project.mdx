When I decided to build an AI-powered Q&A system based on a **single spiritual text**, my goal was not to create a flashy chatbot.

The goal was much stricter:

> Answers must come **only** from the book.  
> No hallucinations. No assumptions. No external knowledge.

This constraint shaped every architectural decision I made—and led me to build my first **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** system.

This post documents what I built, where it broke down, and what this project taught me about **real-world AI systems**, beyond tutorials and demos.

---

## The Problem Statement

At a high level, the system needed to:

- Accept natural language questions
- Retrieve relevant passages from one book
- Generate answers strictly grounded in those passages

Unlike general-purpose chatbots:

- Creativity was a liability
- Confidence without grounding was dangerous
- Saying _“I don’t know based on this text”_ was acceptable—even desirable

This made the project less about models and more about **architecture and discipline**.

---

## Why I Chose RAG Over Fine-Tuning

At first glance, fine-tuning felt like a natural idea:

- One book
- A closed domain
- Repeated themes

But after deeper analysis, **RAG was clearly the better choice**.

### Fine-tuning does not enforce factual grounding

Fine-tuning teaches a model _how to respond_, not _what is true_.

Even a fine-tuned model can hallucinate confidently—especially when asked interpretive or philosophical questions.

For a spiritual text, **faithfulness matters more than fluency**.

---

### RAG keeps the source of truth external

With RAG:

- The LLM does not “own” the knowledge
- The book remains the single source of truth
- Answers can be traced back to retrieved passages

This separation made the system more transparent and easier to reason about.

---

### Content updates are simple

If a translation improves or annotations are added:

- Re-embed the text
- No retraining
- No expensive compute

That flexibility mattered more than squeezing out marginal gains from fine-tuning.

---

## My Initial Chunking Strategy (And Its Limitations)

For this first version, I used **fixed-size chunking**.

The text was split based on character or token length, assuming embedding models would capture sufficient semantic meaning.

This worked—but imperfectly.

---

### What went wrong with fixed-size chunking

1. **Context fragmentation**  
   Ideas were often split mid-thought, weakening retrieval quality.

2. **Partial answers**  
   The retriever surfaced relevant but incomplete passages, leading to shallow responses.

3. **Loss of philosophical continuity**  
   Spiritual texts build meaning gradually. Fixed-size chunks disrupted that flow.

The system did not fail loudly.  
It failed _quietly_—through slightly worse answers.

---

### What I learned (but did not implement yet)

Through experimentation and evaluation, it became clear that **semantic or structure-aware chunking** would significantly improve retrieval quality.

Examples include:

- Chunking complete question–answer sections
- Preserving logical or thematic continuity
- Aligning chunks with the book’s natural structure

Due to time constraints, I chose **not to refactor the entire pipeline** in this iteration.

This became one of my biggest takeaways:

> **Chunking strategy can influence answer quality more than the choice of LLM.**

---

## Retrieval Failures I Didn’t Anticipate

Even with reasonable chunking, retrieval introduced its own challenges.

### Vocabulary mismatch

Users asked questions in modern language, while the text used traditional or philosophical terminology.

For example:

- User: _“How should one control desires?”_
- Text concepts: restraint of senses, detachment, discipline

Embeddings helped—but not always sufficiently.

**Lesson:**  
Retrieval systems must bridge _conceptual gaps_, not just lexical ones.

---

### Too much context hurts performance

Retrieving too many chunks:

- Increased token usage
- Confused the LLM
- Reduced answer precision

In practice, fewer high-quality chunks consistently performed better than flooding the prompt.

---

## Evaluation: The Hardest Part of the Project

Evaluation turned out to be the most underestimated challenge.

In traditional software, outputs are deterministic.

In RAG systems:

- Answers are probabilistic
- Partial correctness is common
- Fluency can mask inaccuracies

Automated metrics offered little insight.

I relied heavily on **manual evaluation**, asking:

- Is the answer strictly grounded in retrieved text?
- Is anything implied or invented?
- Does the model acknowledge uncertainty when needed?

Sometimes, the best answer was simply:

> _“This is not explicitly addressed in the text.”_

---

## What This Project Taught Me About Applied AI

1. **RAG is an architecture, not a shortcut**  
   Every layer—chunking, retrieval, prompting, evaluation—matters.

2. **Retrieval quality beats model size**  
   A smaller model with good context outperformed larger models with weak retrieval.

3. **AI systems should know their limits**  
   Trust increases when a system admits uncertainty.

4. **Constraints improve engineering discipline**  
   Limiting the system to one book prevented shortcuts and forced clarity.

---

## Why This Project Matters to Me

This wasn’t just an AI experiment.

It was an exercise in:

- Respecting the integrity of a sacred text
- Applying modern AI responsibly
- Building systems that prioritize **truth over cleverness**

In an era focused on larger models and broader capabilities, this project reinforced the value of **precision and restraint**.

---

## What I’d Improve in the Next Iteration

- Introduce semantic or structure-aware chunking
- Improve query rewriting for conceptual alignment
- Add explicit citations to retrieved passages
- Build stronger qualitative evaluation workflows

---

## Final Thoughts

If you’re building AI systems for domains where **accuracy, trust, and faithfulness** matter—education, law, medicine, or spiritual texts—RAG is a powerful starting point.

But its success depends far more on **engineering decisions** than on model selection.

This project made one thing clear to me:

> _Good AI systems are built as much with restraint as with intelligence._
